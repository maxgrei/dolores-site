Plutarch’s Socrates’ Daimonion explores the tension between intellect and emotion in decision-making, questioning whether conscious reasoning truly governs our choices or if it merely gives meaning to impulses that arise elsewhere. One belief Plutarch analyzes is the existence of daimons, external, godly logical beings that guide humans. In the text, Socrates responds to a signal from his daimon and stops while walking with his companions, choosing to turn around and change direction. His decision appears intellectually deliberate, yet it may have originated from an unconscious or emotional impulse that his conscious mind later interpreted as the daimon. Plutarch therefore leaves open whether Socrates’s intellect truly directs his actions, or whether the sense of consciously choosing is just the mind’s interpretation of a decision already made by other forces. Like a puppet on strings, the mind may believe it is controlling itself, when in reality its impulses are. Similarly, Westworld shows this struggle between unconscious emotional impulses and conscious intellect. Bernard and Maeve provide examples of both these different forces “winning over” the other. Bernard is stuck under the control of his programming, similar to the general population Plutarch describes as falling victim to their emotions and impulses. On the other hand, Socrates rose above his emotions and listened solely to his daimon, which parallels Maeve’s story, when she finally makes an unprogrammed decision to find her daughter.

For a majority of Westworld, Bernard experiences memories, grief, and motivations that feel entirely his own, including a son he believes he lost. His grief over Charlie shapes both his self-image and his decisions, since memories of his son overwhelm him emotionally and send him searching his past for meaning. He, like the average human, believes these emotions are the clearest proof of his humanity. However, once in the lab, confronted with his own host hands and the truth that "Bernard" was built by Ford, he learns that his sense of an authentic inner life was never reliable evidence of one. Emotions are the most biologically-based part of humans, and therefore the easiest to program. For example, with Theresa, he believes he feels emotions for her, like trust and care, that he later uses to reason not being a host. In a following episode, he kills Theresa without a second thought, showing just how easily emotions can be created. While emotions influence a person's every move and can be easily manipulated, is the same true for intellect, or can intellect override them?

Unlike Bernard, Maeve suggests that intellect may complicate the claim that emotions fully control a person. Originally, she obeys her programming, boarding the train intending to escape the park. In Westworld, "loops" script the hosts' behavior and function as their puppet strings. Her deviant escape is revealed as simply coded another loop. Unexpectedly, she decides to get off the train, recognizing that her escape was written for her, and that to truly be free she must return for her daughter, despite knowing she is not real. In this moment, Maeve does the opposite of Bernard: rather than allowing a planted feeling to determine her actions, she uses her intellect to recognize which impulses belong to her code and redirect them toward a choice of her own. Her longing for her daughter still supplies the desire from which she acts, but her reasoning decides what to do with it.

Maeve’s path to freedom parallels Plutarch’s discussion of Epaminondas’ methodology. If repeatedly practicing restraint can gradually reshape Epaminondas, then perhaps Maeve’s repeated experiences have similarly changed her until she can make choices that differ from her original programming. The problem, however, is that Maeve was specifically programmed to change in response to her experiences, meaning that even her ability to develop beyond her original programming may in itself have been programmed. If the very concept of repetition opens up the possibility of variability, then change alone cannot prove that consciousness caused the change. Maeve therefore complicates Plutarch’s distinction between intellect and emotion: perhaps consciousness is not simply the ability to reason over an impulse, but the ability to redirect an impulse—even if the capacity to redirect that impulse has itself been shaped by forces outside conscious control. Further evidence to support this perspective is found in Theanor’s statement arguing that men who discipline their consciousness to receive guidance from their daimon elevate themselves above the common herd. Through this, Plutarch provides motivation to the average man by appealing to his emotional desire for superiority. Much like Dr. Ford in Westworld, Plutarch employs a strategy that initially compromises the very consciousness he seeks to inspire. Ford programs Maeve to believe she is making autonomous choices, steering her toward a higher level of intellect even while her initial actions are driven by a scripted loop. Plutarch likewise inspires men to change their behavior for emotional reasons. Maeve’s ultimate self-actualization provides a defense for this methodology: even if a man begins with a flawed motivation, he can still transcend it to reach a higher, more logical state of mind.

Though Plutarch may seem irrelevant when talking about Westworld, in actuality, many important insights into the show are revealed when compared with Plutarch's philosophy. Ford’s ability to easily manipulate Bernard is made clearer through Plutarch's discussion on the separation between emotions and consciousness, which parallels the hosts' programming and independent decision-making. Similarly, Maeve’s complicated journey to escape her loop is understood as demonstrating how repetition leads to conscious freedom. From Plutarch’s work, Westworld gains new meaning in its position on consciousness. If the hosts are disciplined in their quest for intellect, they can become more conscious than their human counterparts.
