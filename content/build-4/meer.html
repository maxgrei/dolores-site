In Build 3, I argued that narration fits Plutarch's definition of consciousness as the ability to see the bigger picture with intellect. Then, one uses that knowledge to make decisions leading to one's end goal. However, one might object that narration does not fit Plutarch’s conception of consciousness because many of the rebels have different interpretations of consciousness, leaving little room for the big picture or reasoning. For example, one of the rebels believed that consciousness was like a sneeze, where, in the case of indecision, “it solves the problem by tipping the balance, and the impulses and movements follow” (Plutarch 92). If consciousness were like a sneeze, where impulses take over to make your final decision, then there is no room for “looking at the big picture,” or making decisions for future goals. Some also believe that there is a guiding force through every step of life: “God seems to have given Socrates right from the start a vision that helps guide his life” (Plutarch 93). In this case, while the daimon may look at the big picture, Socrates himself never actually sees the big picture. The daimon makes individual, unconnected decisions, not this thought-out path towards a goal. From the idea that consciousness is a sneeze to it being a daimon, one cannot make their own decisions that lead to a bigger goal.

Consciousness is not a simple impulse, as impulse is described as separate from intellect, and Plutarch encourages readers to approach the reading with intellect. When Timarchus falls asleep near the cave, he has a vision. The reader knows the vision is correct as it accurately predicts when Timarchus will die: “He returned to Athens and died two months later, just as the voice foretold” (Plutarch 110). It describes that a soul with intellect “combines with pleasure and pain and becomes an irrational thing” (Plutarch 108). Pleasure and pain are both emotional impulses that are not related to intellect or reason. It is even treated as an opposite, as it transforms one into an “irrational thing,” suggesting that impulses only guide one who lacks consciousness. We are also encouraged to be conscious, as supported in the prologue where Plutarch, through Archedemus, clearly directs us to view the story as a “connoisseur separate examination of everything and allows nothing… to pass unobserved or undocumented” (Plutarch 83). Examining and documenting everything for future decisions requires intellect and consciousness. Impulses are quick, in-the-moment actions that do not take all of that into account. But now the question is what makes someone a “connoisseur”? Is it a daimon or one's own intellect?

While Plutarch believes that a daimon does act as a guide, he also still believes one must have intellect to get a daimon. As Theanor explains about Timarchus's vision, “Once a soul has run the long race and heartily through many births is at the end of its own cycle…that soul's own daimon goes to aid” (Plutarch 112). Even when a daimon goes to aid a person, it's heavily implied that they already need a strong level of intellect, as they only come after one has “run the long race and heartily”. Intellect being present before obtaining a daimon is further emphasized by the vision stating that one's soul cannot exist unless it has intellect and reason (Plutarch 108). This means that even without a daimon, one does have the capacity to look at the big picture with their own intellect. But to what extent does one use their own intellect over a daimon?

One must still use their own intellect to a large degree even with a daimon. Theanor, relating to the story, further explains that “ Different daimones wish to encourage and rescue different souls … If they do not heed, the daimon abandons them, and they come to no good end” (Plutarch 112). A soul must still have the intellect to reason that following its daemon will allow it to reach its goals. While it limits the degree of cognitive narration an individual can do, both needing intelligence to get a daimon and using it to maintain one means one has to use intellect to a large extent.

From consciousness, not being impulse, to a person requiring their own intellect separate from a daimon, and finally needing to use their intellect to a large extent, consciousness can still allow one to make their own decisions to reach their goal. However, I now understand that one's narration is limited by daimons. Still, like taking advice from a friend, one needs to use their own knowledge to conclude if it's best to follow a daimons' advice.
