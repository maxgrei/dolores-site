At the end of Build 3, I claimed that Plutarch uses reflexivity to mark where the dialogue's accounts of reliable behavior run out. The tiebreaker, trained habit, and confabulation each explain a dependable Socrates until the Crossroads, where he stops "quite suddenly" and remains "silent for some time, concentrating on his own thoughts" (91). By reflexivity I mean a mind taking its own state as the object of its attention, which becomes necessary when impulse alone cannot settle what to do. If the Timarchus myth is right that the intellect sits outside the soul, then attending to one's own mind is how a person in a crisis consults the part of himself that the crisis has not caught up with.

But one might object that reflexivity does not fit Plutarch's conception of consciousness because the dialogue never asks the mind to watch itself, only to listen. Reflexivity and receptivity point attention in opposite directions: a reflexive mind turns inward and makes its own state the object of attention, while a receptive mind turns toward a guide and lets that guide set its course. Every account of the daimonion that the characters take seriously praises the second. The better the soul, the more readily it gives way, and the less it seems to have to examine in itself.

Simmias's account of the daimonion gives the objection its clearest support. He calls it "a perception of a voice" (104), which places its source outside Socrates, and explains that "the mind of a higher being guides the gifted soul by a mere thought, for it needs no blow" (104). The gifted soul "yields to the guide" because its impulses "prove soft and pliant, like reins with some give in them" (104). A soul that must stop and examine itself is offering resistance, which is exactly what Simmias says the gifted soul lacks.

The Timarchus myth cuts the same way. The vision sorts souls by how they move: those with "a straight, orderly movement" were "made responsive to the rein by good breeding and education," while those that "swerved up and down" were "struggling against characters made disobedient" (109). If Socrates is the model inspired man, a sudden halt in the street resembles the swerving of the poorly trained. Socrates himself calls his companions back "by saying that the daimonion had occurred" (91), reporting an event that happened to him. Theanor describes daimones who guide favored souls "by reason, using secret signs" (111), and those souls "listen and are saved" (112). On this account, the silence at the Crossroads is listening, and the thing Socrates attends to is the daimonion.

The objection assumes that listening and self-attention exclude each other, yet the text suggests that one cannot happen without the other. Simmias's guide "slackens or intensifies" the soul's impulses (104). To yield to a guide that works through one's impulses, a soul has to register those impulses changing, so receptivity already requires the soul to take its own state as an object. The pause at the Crossroads also differs from the swerving the myth condemns. The swerving souls move "in a confused and irregular fashion, as though tearing themselves away from their bonds" (109), while Socrates stops, stays still "for some time," and then goes in one direction down Boxmakers Street. His halt looks like a responsive soul feeling the rein, which Plutarch has no reason to treat as a flaw. Timarchus's voice also says that some souls "leave their purest element outside" (108-109), and that element is what those with the right view call daimon. If the daimon is the soul's own purest part, then listening to it is a way of attending to oneself, and "concentrating on his own thoughts" (91) is reflexive and receptive at once.

In Plutarch, reflexivity is a soul turning toward its own highest part when habit and impulse fail, and receptivity is the form that turn takes. Simmias explains how such a turn reaches the body: "insensate bones and sinews ... lie heavy and inert," yet "the moment the soul conceives an idea in the mind and excites its impulses," the whole mass "flies into action" (105). However, if one considers how Plutarch uses reflexivity in order to show a soul consulting its own intellect at the moment trained habit stops acting for it, one can begin to see how reflexivity might allow consciousness to intervene in the stream of physical causality to influence its course. At the Crossroads, a thought Socrates attends to stops his legs and turns him down another street.
