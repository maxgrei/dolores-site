In Build 3, I discussed how reconsolidation, the conscious reevaluation and acceptance of unconscious impulses, offers a quieter form of freedom. However, one might object that retrospective endorsement through reconsolidation does not fit Plutarch’s conception of consciousness. One could say that Plutarch demands an immediate alignment of the soul and intellect at the exact moment of choice, rather than a delayed, reflective rewriting of past impulses. In On the Daimonion of Socrates, Plutarch seems to present the daimonion not as an unconscious drive that is later accepted by the self, but as an instant signal that directly steers action. As Galaxidorus argues, an external sign like a sneeze or a casual word acts on a mind much like an added weight on a scale: "if it is added to one of two opposing calculations, it solves the problem by tipping the balance, and the impulses and movements follow" (92). This sign operates instantly; as the text notes, "one that happened while he [Socrates] was hesitating made him go forward; one that happened in the course of the act gave him pause and stopped his impulse" (92). These examples emphasize how quickly the sign can seemingly create a decision. Simmias adds that enlightened humans yield to a higher mind (daimonion) without friction, describing how "the mind of a higher being guides the gifted soul by a mere thought... Such a soul yields to the guide…" (104). If consciousness consists of such immediate responsiveness to "superior authority and control" (93) at the precise moment an act unfolds, then attempting to salvage freedom after the fact through reconsolidation appears to be mere rationalization. It would be nothing more than an attempt to claim authorship over an unconscious impulse without real conscious intervention.

While this objection rightly highlights the necessity of real-time cognitive control, it overlooks a crucial insight in Plutarch’s text: the daimonion does not inject decisions into a passive mind, but operates on Socrates, whose internal character is already established. A casual sign only tips the scale because "two opposing calculations" (92) already exist in the mind. This enables us to deepen our conception of reconsolidation beyond a simple backward-looking edit. Reconsolidation is not merely an isolated excuse for a past mistake, it is a constant feedback loop that alters the structural "weights" on the balance for all future choices. As Plutarch observes of Socrates, his choices were not "the acts of a man whose resolution changes at the bidding of an accidental word or a sneeze…" (93) but actions guided through by a long-standing "consistency and vigour suggesting that their origin lay in a strong, correct judgement" (92). In describing Socrates this way, Plutarch suggests that his immediate responses reflect an established pattern of judgment. Although the text does not explicitly explain how Socrates developed this character, his “strong, correct judgement” (92) allows us to consider how conscious reflection can shape the unconscious dispositions that later guide decisions.

This idea of a constant reworking of the unconscious is furthered by Epaminondas’ refusal of Theanor's offer of financial gifts. Rather than experiencing a sudden, conflicted internal battle at the moment the gift is presented, Epaminondas immediately refuses–something that stems from a life of conscious, habituated reflection. Caphisias notes that Epaminondas defends their poverty as a "good and loving nurse of youth" (96), insisting on the necessity of distancing oneself from affluence and possible profit to habitually avoid unjust gain (99). Epaminondas's refusal therefore suggests how a consciously examined principle can become an established part of the self, allowing earlier reflection to influence a later decision without requiring new deliberation in the moment. Whereas Socrates illustrates how your established character influences action in a particular moment, Epaminondas suggests how someone might actively cultivate the character that later guides their actions.

So, one might still object that immediate conscious control is necessary for freedom. However, if one considers how Plutarch uses reconsolidation in order to consciously re-evaluate a past memory or unconscious urge, one can begin to see how reconsolidation could modify their unconscious response to those experiences. The next time a similar scenario arises, the brain's impulse is no longer a raw, unexamined drive but one that has been fundamentally re-authored by prior cycles of reflection. Far from a mere illusion of agency, reconsolidation reframes unconscious causation into an evolving, reflective dialogue, creating a quiet and enduring form of freedom that is cultivated over time.
