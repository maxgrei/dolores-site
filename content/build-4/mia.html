Phenomenality treats the felt quality of undergoing an experience as a quality of consciousness. But one might object that phenomenality does not fit Plutarch’s conception of consciousness because he distinguishes undergoing a sign from understanding and answering it correctly, and seemingly values the latter. When Chlidon recounts his wife hiding the bridle from him, his emotions take over his rationale, putting him “quite out of [his] mind” (103). Conversely, Epaminondas, when rejecting Theanor’s gift, is “clear in conscience” (99). Both forms of awareness have a felt character, but phenomenality alone does not explain why the text presents discipline as preferable. However, this distinction does not make phenomenality irrelevant. Rather, Plutarch connects how an experience is undergone to how it is interpreted and answered. Phenomenality does not guarantee correct understanding, but discipline shapes the relationship between felt experience and response.

This objection exposes a limit in the original claim, but does not make phenomenality incompatible with the text. The contrast between Epaminondas and the conspirators could suggest that Plutarch values objective discipline over subjective experience. Yet this assumes that experience can be separated from the habits shaping how a person understands and responds to it. Discipline appears not only in actions but also in how characters experience their circumstances. The question is not whether Epaminondas experiences things “correctly,” but how his cultivated awareness differs from awareness dominated by immediate circumstances. The conspirators’ fear remains relevant despite their unprincipled responses because it shapes how they experience the situation and what they can do in response. Likewise, Chlidon’s feelings are “strangely altered” (103) after the bridle incident, contributing to his being “quite out of [his] mind.” Epaminondas, by contrast, pursues “the right without effort” (99), suggesting that his cultivated disposition prevents competing impulses from overcoming his judgment. Experience does not guarantee correct understanding, but how a person experiences a situation can vary with their condition.

Epaminondas’ explanation of self-control suggests that cultivation changes both actions and the experience of temptation. Explaining his refusal of Theanor’s gift and his choice to remain in poverty, he states, “You have come as an ally to fight poverty, as though we were hard pressed by her. But poverty is easy for us to bear. She is a dear member of the family” (97). Poverty, which another person might experience as deprivation, registers for him as familiar. His description suggests that repeated practice has changed his relationship to poverty, diminishing its power to make him feel deprived and weakening the appeal of material gain. Unlike the conspirators’ rapidly shifting feelings, Epaminondas’ account presents an awareness made steady through repeated practice. Thus, phenomenality is not separate from moral development, since repeated discipline can shape both the felt significance of a situation and the response that follows from it.

Simmias’ account of daimonic guidance further suggests that the soul’s condition shapes how a sign is received and understood, reinforcing the distinction between undergoing an experience and responding to it appropriately (104-105). The sign’s occurrence alone does not guarantee that a person will interpret it correctly or act upon it wisely. Instead, the soul’s preparation influences its capacity to understand and answer what it receives, a capacity that Epaminondas exemplifies through his repeated discipline. He demonstrates principled steadiness when “he would not kill any citizen without fair trial, unless absolutely necessary” despite pressure from his companions (113). His conduct does not reveal precisely what a daimonic sign feels like to him, nor does it establish that he is receiving such guidance in this instance. However, it illustrates how a cultivated disposition can prevent immediate pressure from determining a person’s response. Read alongside Simmias’ account, Epaminondas’ conduct supports the distinction between experiencing something and responding to it appropriately. Discipline does not establish the existence or source of an experience, but it helps explain how a person’s condition can shape its interpretation and consequences.

Rather than abandoning phenomenality as a lens, Plutarch’s text qualifies it, treating felt experience as a dimension of consciousness while showing how discipline shapes its interpretation and response. Felt meaningfulness alone cannot explain why an experience is properly understood or answered. The dialogue’s contrast between fearful confusion and cultivated clarity suggests that disciplined responsiveness has an experiential dimension, even though experience does not guarantee correct judgment. Consciousness can receive what a person does not experience as self-generated, but the interpretation of that experience depends partly on the condition of the soul receiving it. Epaminondas makes phenomenality relevant not because experience guarantees correctness, but because training can reshape what encountering and responding to the world is like.
