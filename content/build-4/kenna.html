Objections to Deliberation in On Socrates' Personal Deity

In my Build 3 essay, I argued that Plutarch’s On Socrates' Personal Deity presents three competing models of consciousness, ultimately favoring a hybrid model where a well-trained mind rationally evaluates and responds to unconscious impulses through deliberation. I focused on how we can experience immediate, uncontrollable impulses and exercise freedom through in-the-moment deliberation. Under this view, conscious freedom occurs through decision making between the initial impulse and the resulting action. However, this interpretation treats unconscious impulses as outside human control. One might object that the text implies that through sustained discipline and repeated practice, individuals can actually reshape their unconscious processes to react logically before conscious deliberation even occurs. Freedom, therefore, is not merely a momentary choice made in response to an impulse; it is the cumulative product of long-term mental discipline that transforms the nature of impulse itself.

In On Socrates' Personal Deity, the conflict between Galaxidorus and Theocritus initially frames deliberation as an immediate choice between opposing options. Galaxidorus views the intellect as an active agent evaluating choices in real time, while Theocritus reduces human action to non-conscious impulses that the mind merely rationalizes after the fact. In my previous analysis, I resolved this tension through Simmias’s mediating position, which suggests that intellect interprets and directs impulses once they arise. Yet this hybrid view still assumes that impulses arise spontaneously and indifferently. It neglects the way character formation conditions the body and mind over time. When Simmias notes that “Socrates’ impulses seem generally to have possessed a consistency and vigor suggesting their origin lay in strong, correct judgement,” and then lists Socrates’ methods of discipline, he implies that Socrates' impulses are not random emotional surges (92). Instead, they are the refined outputs of a mind that has been disciplined over decades. The “superior authority and control” which guides Socrates are his own impulses he has chosen to strengthen over time (93). Socrates' daimonion feels intuitive because his conscious rational principles have become his unconscious habits.

Epaminondas’s actions throughout the dialogue further demonstrate how continuous training alters unconscious inclinations rather than merely restraining them in critical moments. Epaminondas refuses to participate in the rebellion, citing his obligation not to harm fellow citizens and his desire to stabilize Thebes after the coup (112–113). Viewed through my previous lens of moment-to-moment deliberation, Epaminondas experiences a conflict from random impulse, ultimately choosing the rational path through momentary conscious deliberation. However, his ability to refrain from violence is not an isolated act of willpower exerted under pressure. It is the outcome of a lifetime spent practicing restraint and “habituating himself to keep away from unjust or unlawful gain” (99). By consistently choosing restraint in minor matters, Epaminondas trains his mind so that, when confronted with a crisis, his immediate inclination is rational. His feeling of obligation is not an arbitrary, unchosen impulse he must struggle against; it is an impulse cultivated by his own prior choices. Then his following deliberation is guided by the impulse and requires less internal struggle.

This model of a trainable impulse alters how I understand human agency and moral responsibility in Plutarch's work. If freedom only existed during in-the-moment deliberation, human choice would remain perpetually vulnerable to the chaos of sudden, overwhelming impulses. By contrast, Plutarch suggests that true agency is prospective: we exercise freedom when we choose which habits, disciplines, and thought patterns to cultivate over time. Socrates does not simply pause on a street corner to deliberate after receiving a sign from his daimonion; his lifelong dedication to virtue ensures that his mind registers subtle, rational cues that others would ignore. Likewise, Epaminondas does not need to wage an internal war against impulse during the uprising because his past discipline has already aligned his moral instincts with logical outcomes.

Ultimately, my interpretation of consciousness in Plutarch’s text is no longer a simple choice between rational deliberation and unconscious impulse. Unlike I thought before, unconscious impulse can actually be consciously trained. While Epaminondas may not choose his initial feeling of obligation, he has spent a lifetime choosing the discipline that produced that feeling. Deliberation in the moment is merely the surface expression of a much deeper, long-term training of the soul. True freedom is not the frantic evaluation of choices when a crisis arrives, but the quiet, continuous work of shaping our unconscious mind so that our immediate inclinations naturally reflect our rational principles.
