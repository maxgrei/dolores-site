Both Plutarch and Milton tell stories, one about philosophy within the wider context of the liberation of Thebes from Sparta and the other about Adam and Eve’s Fall. While these stories are very different, Plutarch and Milton both show that instantaneous choices do not decide whether you have conscious agency. Rather, they use the idea of reconsolidation, that one can continuously reevaluate their past, present, and future to make changes to the self. However, they differ in the sense that Adam and Eve merely recognize the freedom possible within reconsolidation whereas Epaminondas actually uses reconsolidation to create a constant and circular dialogue with his unconscious.

Plutarch and Milton both reject the idea of split-second decisions determining free will. Instead, they seem to view consciousness through the idea of reconsolidation, meaning that the way you interpret and consciously act on those unconscious choices determines consciousness. Plutarch demonstrates this idea through Epaminondas, who incorporates his past teachings about poverty from Lysis into his very being, building a strong sense of discipline over the years. Specifically, Lysis teaches Epaminondas about how poverty should be treated as a gift. Although Epaminondas did not choose to adhere to these ideas as a child, he can still take some control over it through his daily conscious acceptance of those unconscious beliefs. Similarly, Milton uses Adam and Eve after the Fall to show how their past faults, through reconsolidation, influence their future. After the Fall, Adam and Eve initially shut down as guilt and sin instantly settled into their unconscious minds. However, while they didn’t purposefully choose such extreme knowledge, they now accept and internalize it, recognizing that reconsolidation allows them to redefine it. Instead of falling victim to their unconscious desire to sin, they can consciously assign this knowledge a new purpose: the chance to do good.

However, reconsolidation shapes the conscious minds of Adam and Eve versus Epaminondas very differently. Milton structures Adam and Eve’s reconsolidation not as a cyclical, daily exercise to manage the unconscious, but as a linear transformation from a single event. Before the Fall, Adam and Eve existed in a blissful innocence. Once they fall, they momentarily become puppets to their new sinful desires, giving into lust immediately. However, they recognize reconsolidation as a way for them to regain some free will and redeem themselves. Archangel Michael enlightens them to this, saying: "Only add / Deeds to thy knowledge answerable, add faith, / Add virtue, patience, temperance, add love, / By name to come called charity, the soul / Of all the rest…” (581-585). Once they see that they can bring good into the world because of their new knowledge, they stop feeling like mere puppets to their unconscious impulses to sin. Archangel Michael demonstrates how they can consciously absorb and refashion their unconscious reactions, accepting them and reinterpreting them as an opportunity to create positive change in the world. In the end, Adam and Eve consciously venture away from their paradise, welcoming a world where they can use the good traits the knowledge has allowed them to recognize in themselves for good. They look forward to finding future freedom through their recognition of the power that reconsolidation can give them, enabling conscious mastery over their unconscious mind.

On the other hand, Plutarch uses Epaminondas to show someone who practices reconsolidation in an ongoing, cyclical dialogue that seems to actively change his unconscious mind. Specifically, Epaminondas describes how he applies his mental discipline to one main aspect of his life: his poverty. After Epaminondas refuses Theanor’s offer of compensation for his care of Lysis, he explains his actions to Theanor: "No; the man who voluntarily and repeatedly distances himself from perfectly just and permissible sources of profit is also the man who is really exercising and habituating himself to keep away from unjust or unlawful gain" (99). Epaminondas, like Adam and Eve, reinterprets his unconscious beliefs, instead seeing them as a path to who he genuinely wants to be. He differs though, in that he takes the ideas of poverty he learned from Lysis and consciously turns them inward consistently, “practicing poverty” daily until it truly becomes a part of his unconscious. This gives him a form of free will, as his conscious discipline now constantly influences his unconscious decisions.

Yet, beneath these differences lies a common insight, namely that reconsolidation allows one to gain some form of control over their unconscious. In a way, Epaminondas acts as the more developed, or later, version of Adam and Eve, if their story continued. Within the range of Milton’s story, they simply recognized the power of reconsolidation and started their journey to use it in the future. However, if the story went on, one can imagine that they would actively and cyclically deliberate like Epaminondas. This shared perspective complicates the idea of free will by showing that true human agency never comes down to a single choice. Instead, past reflection affects our real-time decisions, just as our present reflection actively changes our unconsciousness for future decisions. Ultimately, reconsolidation takes unconscious drives, external influences, and even catastrophic mistakes and subjects them to conscious evaluation, creating an elastic and ever-changing unconscious over time.
