In Build 4, I argued that consciousness may influence our behavior by helping us develop habits of self-control. However, this raises another question: if our experiences shape our desires and decisions, how much control do we have over the way we respond to them? Both Plutarch and Milton explore how outside influences shape people's desires and behavior. In Plutarch's Timarchus myth, experiences of pleasure and pain can change the soul without a conscious decision. Similarly, Milton depicts Satan influencing Eve's reasoning by appealing to her desire for knowledge. Both authors suggest that our decisions can be shaped by influences we did not choose. However, while Plutarch presents consciousness as a possible way to develop control over these influences, Milton shows how conscious reasoning can itself become influenced by temptation.

In Plutarch's Timarchus myth, the part of the soul connected to the body and its passions can be changed by pleasure and pain "into an irrational thing" (108). This suggests that experiences can shape someone's desires and behavior without their consciously choosing to change. However, Plutarch also presents the possibility that consciousness can influence how someone responds to those desires. When Epaminondas explains why he refuses Theanor's money, he argues that someone who "voluntarily and repeatedly distances himself from perfectly just and permissible sources of profit" is "exercising and habituating himself to keep away from unjust or unlawful gain" (98–99). Epaminondas recognizes that accepting the money would not be wrong, but believes refusing it will help him resist more serious temptations in the future. Rather than simply reacting to his desires, he considers how his present actions might shape his future behavior. He argues that someone who repeatedly practices restraint will eventually "pursue the right without effort" (99). This suggests that consciousness may help someone develop habits that eventually guide their actions without conscious effort. Still, Epaminondas's decision to practice restraint may itself reflect values shaped by his upbringing and past experiences. His conscious understanding of why restraint is beneficial does not necessarily mean that understanding caused him to practice it.

Milton explores a similar relationship between outside influences and conscious decisions, but presents a different possibility for how consciousness responds to temptation. Satan persuades Eve to reconsider God's command by suggesting that eating the forbidden fruit will give her greater knowledge, promising that she will "be as gods, / Knowing both good and evil as they know" (IX.708–709). Rather than forcing Eve to disobey, Satan appeals to her desire for understanding and encourages her to question whether eating the fruit is actually wrong. Eve considers his arguments and reasons that the fruit could benefit her, despite knowing that God had forbidden it. She begins questioning God's command herself, asking, "what forbids he but to know? / Forbids us good, forbids us to be wise?" (IX.758–759). Unlike Epaminondas, who uses his understanding of temptation to practice restraint, Eve works through her own reasoning to justify giving in to it. Her questions suggest that Satan's argument has influenced how she understands God's command, which she now sees as preventing her from gaining knowledge. Although she consciously decides to eat the fruit, the reasoning behind her decision has been shaped by an outside influence. Milton therefore suggests that conscious reasoning can reinforce temptation by helping someone rationalize giving in. This complicates Plutarch's account because it suggests that conscious reasoning alone may not be enough to resist temptation.

Despite these differences, both Plutarch and Milton suggest that our conscious decisions are influenced by experiences and desires we do not fully control. However, their different responses to temptation suggest that consciousness may still play a role in shaping future behavior. Eve shows how conscious reasoning can justify giving in to temptation, which helps explain why Epaminondas believes repeated practice is necessary. If understanding what is right were enough to guarantee virtuous behavior, he would have little reason to refuse permissible wealth. Instead, he tries to develop habits that make resisting temptation easier, even when his desires might encourage him to act otherwise. This changes my understanding of reconsolidation from Build 4. I previously argued that consciousness may shape future behavior by helping someone develop habits of self-control. Comparing Plutarch and Milton has made me consider that consciousness can help us recognize and resist our desires, but it can also provide reasons to follow them. Reconsolidation may therefore involve not only recognizing how our experiences influence us, but also developing habits that make us less dependent on conscious reasoning when faced with temptation.
