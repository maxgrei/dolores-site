Both Plutarch and Plato believe narrative consciousness (the ability to choose actions that lead to a virtuous soul in the long term) is necessary in becoming virtuous. However, Plutarch believes that once a soul has mastered this aspect of consciousness, a spirit called a daimon helps guide it. In contrast, Plato believes that once one has mastered it and dies, a daimon guides one to complete freedom from needing consciousness. Both believe that after attaining virtue, narrative consciousness is less necessary.

Both Plutarch and Plato assert one must make decisions that lead to a virtuous soul through Socrates' explanation and Epaminondas' denial of wealth. As Socrates teaches Simmias right before his death, “If a soul is immortal we must care for… for the sake of all time” (Plato 107c-d). He later clarifies that “care” refers to making one's soul as intelligent and virtuous as possible (Plato 107c-d). Caring for one's soul over multiple lifetimes is like the goal-oriented thinking Epaminondas exhibits. He consciously refuses money to pursue a more virtuous life: “Do we give up our poverty for wealth…no indeed… poverty is our ‘good and loving nurse '” (Plutarch 96). Clarifying that poverty is good implies wealth is bad, so choosing poverty would be an act of virtue. Through Socrates' explanation and Epaminondas actions, both decide on actions that nurture their souls. With their goal so similar, it might lead one to believe all aspects that appear in both readings are similar, including that of a daimon.

In actuality, they differ greatly: Plutarch believes a supernatural force called a daimon guides one's consciousness during lifetimes. Throughout Plutachs story, many interpretations of a daimon and how it relates to consciousness. For the sake of brevity, once a soul has been good over the span of many lives (Plutarch 112), a spirit called a daimon chooses that person to “guide his own life” (Plutarch 100). A daimon does not guide one in the afterlife; instead, it guides while one is alive. A person still retains some autonomy, as they can choose not to listen to it, granted it comes with the risk of death: “If they do not heed, the daimon abandons them, and they come to no good end” (Plutarch 112). This still does hinder cognitive decision-making, as instead of thinking through each step, one must only listen to a daimon. One may ask how this differs from Plato?

Plato believes that a daimon sending one to a pure place makes narrative consciousness unnecessary through his pure world. Plato, through Socrates, highlights how in the afterlife a daimon can guide souls to be judged for those who have lived virtuously, “are freed and released from regions of the earth… they make their way up to a pure dwelling place” (Plato 114c). A pure dwelling place contrasts with Plutarch's imperfect world humans are trapped navigating. In Plato's “pure” world, one does not need to navigate with narrative consciousness; as Plato mentions earlier, he believes in “absolute good and absolute beauty” (Plato 65e-d). If there exists a “pure” place, then it comprises both these aspects. In a world of all good, there is no corruption. If the only way to live is virtuously, narrative consciousness is completely unnecessary. This may lead one to believe that the ideas on consciousness differ greatly. In what way could these two opposing ideas connect?

Both believe that once one has mastered a virtuous soul through practice, narrative consciousness becomes secondary. Plutarch, through Epaminondas, explains that one can exercise virtue like a muscle; as one practices it, achieving it becomes easier. (Plutarch 98) Similarly, Plato also subscribes to the idea of practicing virtue leading to ease: “if it has pursued philosophy in the right way and really practiced how to face death easily” (Plato 80d-81t). It's only once someone has practiced virtue to a high degree that either a daimon guides or sends someone to the pure place. In this case, they both represent ease of virtue by requiring little to no narrative consciousness. Plato's belief is indeed much more extreme than Plutarch's, but both stem from the idea that continual practice of virtue requires less effort from a conscious once one has become virtuous.

At a surface level, Plato and Plutarch share ideas on one's own ability to narrate but diverge on the role narrative consciousness plays after attaining one's goals. Looking deeper, both believe that once one obtains virtue through narrative consciousness, that ability becomes less necessary. If consciousness is meant to help one reach a goal, then is there any use in it after achieving said goal? Maybe consciousness isn't the end goal, but rather a stepping stone left behind when reaching our goal.
