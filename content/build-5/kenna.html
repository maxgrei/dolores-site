Deliberation in On Socrates' Personal Deity and Paradise Lost

In On Socrates' Personal Deity and Paradise Lost, Plutarch and John Milton similarly explore deliberation as the essential mechanism through which human beings exercise conscious freedom within restraint. Rather than viewing human action as either entirely deterministic or entirely free, both authors place their characters within constrained causal fields. Characters like Epaminondas and Eve demonstrate that conscious freedom can exist within these limits by prioritizing second-order desires over first-order impulses. Yet the two works display differences in the role of impulse and our ability to reshape our unconscious processes. Milton’s work implies everyone possesses the innate, equal ability to reason in the moment. Plutarch expands beyond this, implying that the capacity for deliberation can be trained over time and lead some people to be more consciously free and virtuous than others. Their combined perspectives imply all people are capable of choosing their paths in life; however, the capability to do so exists on a spectrum.

My understanding of deliberation in past drafts is the ability to rationally respond to emotional impulses by choosing a path from the causal field in alignment with long-term second-order desires. Milton presents this ability as an innate, universal rational gift bestowed at creation, establishing a baseline floor of freedom shared equally by all conscious beings. Eve’s choice to eat the forbidden fruit requires clear logical reasoning as the serpent must work hard to convince her. Eve actively weighs future consequences and evaluates counterfactual claims like when she questions God’s warning that the fruit will lead to death, questioning the reality of mortality itself by asking, "Was death invented?" (IX.767). Eventually she determines eating the apple will help her fulfill second-order desires for knowledge and godly power, a decision combining logic and the emotional impulse of greed. Eve’s decision is a bad one and leads to the fall of Eden. For Milton, deliberation grants every person equal access to baseline conscious freedom, but does not imply that deliberative skills can be improved over time.

Is this baseline deliberative ability limited to momentary logic, or can people improve their ability to make virtuous decisions over time? My interpretation of Plutarch is one that treats deliberation as a trained skill that expands conscious freedom over time. Plutarch suggests that habituation through practiced restraint aids in deliberation, allowing those who train themselves to not only deliberate but make more virtuous, moral decisions. When Epaminondas refuses a financial gift on the grounds that resisting small temptations "arms the soul against the forbidden," his restraint demonstrates how minor deliberate choices alter future unconscious impulses (99). Although he cannot control the external presentation of the gift (the causal field) his choice to reject it serves his second-order commitment to avoid "unjust or unlawful gain" (99). Over time, this repeated mental training embeds conscious virtuous principles into automatic habit, unlike those who have not practiced the same. I interpret the rulers in Thebes as examples of characters who have not practiced restraint, similar to Eve. They become tyrants because their ability to rationally deliberate is overridden by their emotional impulse for power (84). For Plutarch, real-time decision-making is a momentary expression of a long-term training of the soul, demonstrating that individuals who habituate their minds achieve a higher degree of moral conscious freedom than those relying solely on momentary willpower.

Combining these differences gives me a developed definition of deliberation in which logical reasoning is present in all people but can be improved on, leading to a progressive spectrum of conscious freedom. While Milton’s innate reason grants us baseline freedom in the moment, it is still likely to be influenced by bad impulses. His work establishes that human beings are never entirely deterministic, because the innate capacity to deliberate after impulse ensures that every individual can evaluate choices and choose their path. Plutarch’s long-term deliberative practice expands our capacity for true self-governance over time by helping us better keep in line with our second-order desires. Trained impulses make people more likely to make good decisions; we are not locked into our initial level of agency. By using real-time deliberation to repeatedly choose virtue, we systematically retrain our unconscious processes, ensuring that our immediate inclinations naturally reflect our highest moral principles. Together, Plutarch and Milton lead me to see deliberation as a tool for all people to use and become more consciously free through the practice of restraint.
