An object’s significance extends beyond its appearance, shaped by the experience of its observer. Emerging through lived experience, phenomenality reveals how consciousness responds to and shapes the world. In Socrates’ Daimonion, Plutarch examines how the soul receives and interprets external guidance, while in The Interior Structure of the Artifact, Scarry argues that objects embody the human experiences that produce them. However, the two differ in where significance originates. Plutarch traces significance from external guidance to internal interpretation, whereas Scarry traces it from internal human experience to external physical form. Read together, their arguments reveal that consciousness is not simply the relationship between internal experience and external influences, but a cycle in which each continually shapes and reshapes the other.

Both authors locate significance in the interaction between internal experience and the external world. For Plutarch, external guidance must be received and interpreted by the soul before it acquires personal meaning. Galaxidorus argues that a sneeze or casual word “cannot on its own determine a weighty mind to action” (Plutarch, 92), though it can tip the balance as the mind weighs options. The daimonic sign gains significance through an already deliberating consciousness. Simmias adds that “such a soul yields to the guide” (104), showing that external guidance takes effect because the soul is receptive. Similarly, for Scarry, an object’s physical structure embodies the human experience that produced it, extending that experience beyond the individual. A chair, for example, takes “the shape of perceived-pain-wished-gone” (Scarry, 290), giving material form to a person’s discomfort and desire for relief. Because that experience now exists outside the individual, the object can act back on others as a “lever” that “remakes the makers” (307), shaping how they experience the world. The object simultaneously embodies and reshapes human experience. In both accounts, significance depends on an exchange between inner experience and the outer world.

However, the authors differ in the initial movement of phenomenality. Plutarch treats phenomenality as the reception of external guidance. Significance originates in a force beyond the individual, and the soul is the medium through which that force enters consciousness. Plutarch explains that “the mind of a higher being guides the gifted soul by a mere thought” (104), conveying how external guidance influences the soul without physical intervention. The soul “yields” but its response is not automatic. Timarchus’ vision describes it as “sometimes successfully guiding their course to the right, sometimes twisted by emotion and dragged down by wrongdoing, and then making further efforts to resist and force their way up” (109). The soul's varying responses indicate that it is not passive in receiving external guidance, but actively shapes its influence through its own impulses and resistance. Although significance originates externally, its effect depends on the soul's internal condition and response.

While Plutarch begins with external guidance entering internal experience, Scarry examines how human experience is projected outward into physical objects, making the initial movement of significance internal-to-external. For instance, Scarry argues that a woman making a coat is not interested in the garment itself, but that “her skilled attention to threads, materials, seams, linings are all objectifications of the fact that she is at work to remake human tissue to be free of the problem of being cold” (307). The coat gives physical form to the woman’s experience of needing warmth, extending her sentience into the material world. It is not the goal but the means by which the maker’s experience of cold is addressed. However, this movement is not one-directional. Scarry explains that the object’s “power now moves back from the object realm to the human realm where sentience itself is remade” (307). Human experience gives the object its form, while the completed object transforms subsequent human experience.

Plutarch and Scarry approach phenomenality from opposite starting points. Plutarch examines how external forces enter consciousness, while Scarry explores how internal experiences take physical form. Considered together, these directions form a continuous cycle of phenomenality. External influences enter and shape internal experience, which is then projected outward into the world and takes form in objects that shape subsequent experiences. Plutarch's account emphasizes how the soul's internal condition shapes its response to external guidance, while Scarry's shows how objects created through human experience reshape the experiences of others. By combining these directions, their arguments challenge a strict division between internal consciousness and external reality. Consciousness is therefore an ongoing cycle in which individuals are shaped by the world while simultaneously reshaping it through their experiences.
