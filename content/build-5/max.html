Plutarch's sneeze episode and Descartes's wax argument both test whether what the senses report can, on its own, determine what the mind knows or does. Each case has an object, the sneeze or the wax, and a subject, the mind that receives it, and both authors locate meaning in the subject. According to one rumor, Socrates' daimonion was a sneeze, and the same sneeze meant opposite things depending on Socrates' state: one "while he was hesitating made him go forward," while one "in the course of the act gave him pause" (92). Descartes watches a piece of wax by the fire lose its taste, smell, color, and shape (20), yet still calls it the same wax. In both cases the object settles nothing by itself, and what turns it into knowledge or action is the subject looking back at its own state, which is what I call reflexivity. The authors part ways over what that reflexive subject should rely on: its senses, its own judgment, or a guide outside itself.

The first difference lies in how much weight each author gives the body's report. For Descartes, none of the qualities his senses reported survived the fire, so the wax must be grasped by "purely mental scrutiny" (21). Galaxidorus, by contrast, says a sneeze "cannot on its own determine a weighty mind to action," but added to "one of two opposing calculations" it tips the balance (92). The difference comes from what each author assumes the mind is doing when the signal arrives. Descartes's subject is trying to know what something is, and for that purpose the senses only distract, because the qualities they report keep changing. Galaxidorus's subject is trying to choose between two actions it has already weighed, and for that purpose a trivial sign can help.

But where does the reflexive turn lead once the mind looks past the senses? In the Second Meditation, Descartes ends inside his own mind: the wax ends up teaching him "the nature of my own mind" (22). Socrates' turn leads outward. The sneeze story is only a rumor that Polymnis rejects, but the rumor and Polymnis agree on one point. The rumor says Socrates "always said that the daimonion was stopping him or leading him on" (92), and Polymnis, defending him, calls him a man guided "towards the good by some superior authority and control" (93). Both accounts have Socrates treating the source of his decision as something acting on him from outside. Descartes does turn to God in the Third Meditation, but only to secure what lies beyond the cogito, so the difference is one of order. Descartes trusts his own mind first and an outside guarantor second, while Socrates looks at his own mind to recognize when something beyond it is speaking.

Yet, beneath these differences lies a common insight, namely that a bodily event only becomes meaningful once the mind has checked its own condition. The sneeze rules work only if Socrates knows whether he is hesitating or already acting, and the wax stays the same wax only after Descartes notices that his judgment, and not his eyes, is doing the work. This extends my claim from Build 4, where reflexivity was a soul turning toward its own highest part when habit fails: reflexivity is also how the mind sorts the body's signals before acting on them. I find this convincing because it explains the strangest detail of the rumor, which Build 1 could only call fine print: the same sneeze needed opposite rules because its meaning was never in the sneeze.

Plutarch and Descartes both use reflexivity in order to show a mind turning toward its own highest part when habit and the senses can no longer settle what to do. Yet, how can reflexivity allow consciousness to intervene in the stream of physical causality and influence its course? A sneeze is the same physical event each time, yet it sends Socrates forward when he is hesitating and stops him when he is already acting. What decides the outcome is his awareness of his own state, a fact about the mind's relation to itself that the sneeze cannot supply. If the same cause can produce opposite effects depending on how the mind stands toward its own condition, then the cause alone does not determine the result, at least as Plutarch presents it. Consciousness takes hold of physical causes at a specific point: the pause between a bodily input and the body's response, where the mind, by reading its own state, finds somewhere to stand outside the stream of causes and decides which way the input will push.
