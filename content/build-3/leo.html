In Build 1, I understood Epaminondas’s repeated acts of restraint mainly as training that strengthens his ability to resist future temptations. On rereading Plutarch, I began to see this process through reconsolidation, the way consciousness selects past experiences, present circumstances, and future projections to navigate changes in who someone is. Epaminondas believes that past habits can strengthen present desires. So, he practices restraint because of how he expects that practice to affect him later. This initially made me think reconsolidation gives consciousness control over change. However, the Timarchus myth’s exploration of how pleasure and pain transform the soul made me reconsider this interpretation. If experiences shape desires without conscious choice, perhaps consciousness does not initiate change but rather recognizes and influences a process already underway.

To understand how Epaminondas attempts to consciously shape his future character, it is important to consider how he believes desires develop. Epaminondas explains that unnecessary desires can “acquire force and strength as a result of long habit and education” (98). Repeatedly giving in to them can therefore make them more difficult to resist later. Epaminondas argues that, to prevent this, people should practice restraining desires before they become difficult to control later: someone who “voluntarily and repeatedly distances himself from perfectly just and permissible sources of profit” is “exercising and habituating himself to keep away from unjust or unlawful gain” (98–99). Accepting permissible profit is not wrong, but Epaminondas refuses it because he anticipates how his present choices will shape his future desires; in doing so, he attempts to direct his own character development. I initially took this as evidence that he could deliberately change himself through conscious practice.

However, his past experiences may have already shaped the desires he is now trying to control. The Timarchus myth complicates my understanding of Epaminondas because it demonstrates how a person can be shaped by experiences without consciously choosing how those experiences affect them. In the myth, Timarchus leaves his body and observes what happens to souls when they become connected to the body and its passions. He learns that although every soul possesses reason or intellect, the part of the soul connected with the body “is changed by pleasure and pain into an irrational thing” (108). This suggests that pleasure and pain can give emotions greater influence over someone without that person intentionally choosing this change. 4 Comparing the process Timarchus observes with Epaminondas’s practice made me reconsider consciousness’s real role in change. Consciousness may not determine which desires Epaminondas initially experiences, but reconsolidation enables him to recognize how past experiences have influenced those desires and choose whether to reinforce or resist them.

If consciousness can influence a process of change that it did not begin, conscious control may not require control over where impulses come from. Simmias explains that a higher being can guide a soul by “slacken[ing] or intensif[ying] its impulses,” which remain “soft and pliant, like reins with some give in them” (104). The reins suggest that control does not require creating the force being controlled. Although Simmias describes a higher being guiding the soul rather than the soul guiding itself, this resembles Epaminondas’s attempt to control desires that already exist. He may not choose which desires arise, but he can practice resisting them so they have less influence over his later actions. Initially, I thought that if Epaminondas did not choose his desires, then his later restraint could not show that consciousness was changing him. On rereading, I realized that he may not control which desires arise, but he can still influence how he responds to them.

Yet the ability to resist an existing desire does not explain why someone chooses restraint in the first place. This possibility changes the question I raised in Build 1 about whether Epaminondas practiced restraint because he was already a virtuous, self-disciplined person. If his decision to practice restraint came from past habits and experiences, consciousness may not independently determine his actions, even when he believes he is consciously choosing them. However, this does not mean consciousness has no influence over his actions. Through Epaminondas, Timarchus, and Simmias, Plutarch suggests that consciousness enables someone to recognize and redirect impulses without necessarily determining those impulses in the first place. Reconsolidation gives consciousness a role in shaping future behavior, even when the decisions someone makes are influenced by past experiences.
