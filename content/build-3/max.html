In Build 1, I treated the exchange on page 92 as one case against Socrates, when in fact its two speakers argue different things. Polymnis reports the sneeze rules and then rejects them himself, calling that kind of panic "vain pretention" (92). I had taken his point about Socrates' consistency as support for confabulation when it was his reason to doubt the sneeze. Galaxidorus isn't accusing Socrates of invention either. A trivial sign, he says, "cannot on its own determine a weighty mind to action," but it can tip the balance between "two opposing calculations" (92). His version of Socrates is already weighing options before any sneeze arrives.

The Timarchus myth is a bigger problem, because it reverses what confabulation assumes: a real cause inside the person and an outside force invented afterward. The voice Timarchus hears says most people believe their intellect is inside them, the way they believe reflections are inside mirrors. "Those who have the right view, however, call it daimon, and know it is outside them" (109). On that account the mistaken story is the one that puts the cause inside. Simmias does call all this "myth or fiction rather than argument" (106), so it isn't Plutarch's verdict. Even as myth, it still shows that the inside/outside line in Build 1 was one I drew myself.

Epaminondas raises a different problem: he behaves reliably and reports no sign, a case Build 1 never had to account for. To Theanor he talks only about practice: "habit and exercise often enable reason to draw off some of the strength of the innate passions" (98), until a trained man "will pursue the right without effort" (99). It is Theanor who decides afterward that he "had the same daimon to guide his own life" (100). The same conduct gets two descriptions, so the debate shifts from whether the sign was real to which mechanism makes a person dependable. And "without effort" means a trained response doesn't require him to notice anything.

That gives three ways to explain a reliable Socrates (a tiebreaker, trained habit, and confabulation), and the Crossroads scene doesn't sit comfortably in any of them. A tiebreaker needs a sign and a mind already split between two options, and neither is reported. Trained habit runs without effort and has no reason to stop him in the street. Confabulation might explain the label he gives afterward but not the silence before it. What's left is reflexivity: awareness of one's own awareness, as opposed to awareness of the street or the conversation, at a moment when impulse can't settle the decision.

What does that look like in the scene itself? Theocritus has Socrates walking toward the Crossroads "confusing Euthyphro in his playful way," his attention on other people. "Then, quite suddenly, he stopped and was silent for some time, concentrating on his own thoughts" (91). "Pensive" would be the easy word, but a pensive person has chosen to reflect, and here the stop comes first and the concentrating follows. "Interrupted" is closer. Simmias later says that when the thinking mind "is disturbed, the strings are pulled, and in turn draw and pull upon the man" (105), and that is the order here: something disturbs his thinking and he stops before he has worked anything out. The reflexive part is "for some time," when the thing he attends to is his own mind. Only then does he turn down Boxmakers Street and call the others back, and that is a decision. Is the silence its own category, a mind watching itself? Or is it Galaxidorus's scale seen from the inside, or Epaminondas's training at the one moment it fails to run on its own?

Those questions point to the claim I will develop in Build 4. Plutarch surrounds the Crossroads scene with rival accounts of reliable behavior and lets each one fail at the moment Socrates stops. He uses reflexivity to mark where they run out: a mind disturbed in the middle of acting has to attend to its own state before it can act. The reason may lie in the Timarchus myth, where the intellect sits outside the soul it steers. Reflexivity is the closest a living person comes to that position, and Plutarch sets it in a crisis because that is when trained habit can no longer act for him.
