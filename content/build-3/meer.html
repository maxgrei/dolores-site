Originally, I believed Plutarch claimed that emotions needed to be suppressed to pursue intellect. While this simple interpretation captures a basic idea, it misses nuance in the text. Through the lens of narration, Plutarch argues that consciousness requires active decision-making aimed at a larger long-term goal. This is shown through Epaminondas ' control of himself, his control over others leading to his objectives, and, in contrast, the rebels' lack of control jeopardizing their mission.

Epaminondas controls his greed through intellect to ensure he stays self-sufficient in the future. When Epaminondas' mentor dies, he and his family are offered money for their acts of service. Epaminondas denies this offer, explaining that “ poverty is easy for us to bear…we need no money to fight what causes us no trouble” (Plutarch 97). At first, one may believe this decision was made to live more virtuously. While this is not entirely wrong, it misses Plutarch's deeper argument. When one gets money, the desire to spend as much as possible becomes uncontrollable. Emphasized by Epaminondas when he frames giving money as an unwanted lure to something worse: “He was using money to tempt a private citizen” (Plutarch 97). Epaminondas's intention is clear: by denying money, he controls himself into poverty to reach his ultimate goal of frugality. Clearly, something he desires, as he's glad his mentor was “teaching us not to complain about poverty” (Plutarch 97). But how far does this control go? Is it only for one's own life, or can it control others to reach one's own aspiration?

Plutarch emphasizes that using intellect to see the big picture allows one to consciously control people around them. Initially, Epaminondas is criticized for not slaughtering people without due justice to aid the rebellion (Plutarch 85). Later, the decision was revealed as deliberate. Intellect allowed him to see the bigger picture. He knew that when the crowd panicked and had no one to trust, he could swoop in and save the day, with no blood on his hands; he appeared trustworthy. This allowed him to calm and command the crowd (Plutarch 119). This is no coincidence either. Earlier in the story, the plan would have fallen through because the rebels forgot about their own plan while discussing politics. But he reminded them: “Epaminondas looked at me. ‘Time for you to go to the gymnasium’ [Where their plan takes place]”(Plutarch 112). Why would Plutarch remind the rebels to commit to their plan when he doesn't want soldiers to die without a fair trial? Knowing his ultimate dream is restoring a liberated Thebes (Plutarch 119). It becomes clear that he used his consciousness to control others, reminding them to continue their plan and leaving his hands bloodless to govern the crowd. Plutarch shows that one can control both themselves and others through consciousness. But is this really what consciousness is? Some may argue that consciousness is defined in many ways throughout the story, mostly by the rebels. Some mention that it is an impulse; others theorize it could be an outside voice, and some even believe that voice is divine (Plutarch 92-93). So it's impossible to pin down a single definition. Or is it?

At first, it seems there are many definitions of consciousness, but the rebels' failure to execute their plan reveals their definitions are untrustworthy. All definitions agree that consciousness accomplishes a goal, but how is up for debate. So, to judge other definitions of consciousness means seeing whether they accomplish their objective. The rebels' mission is liberating Thebes by killing the tyrants. But their plan was a complete mess because of the forms of consciousness they believe in. When one of the rebels confronts the other for not having the nerve to start the rebellion, in time it “threw all us[rebels] in a panic”(Plutarch 101). Not only does one rebel's impulse delay the coup, but it also throws them into an even bigger frenzy. As mentioned earlier, they had to be reminded of their plan (Plutarch 112). They believed consciousness was an outside voice guiding them. Yet no outside voice or deity led them to their goal. No, it was Epaminondas. In both scenarios, Plutarch uses their views of consciousness, constantly failing to place doubt on those views. While it is still possible for consciousness to have slight variation in definition, it certainly is not as diverse as it first seems.

From Epaminondas using his consciousness to achieve his future aspirations by guiding himself, to controlling others, and finally the rebels failing through their definitions of consciousness, Plutarch indicates that consciousness is decisions made with intentional control to reach a final goal.
