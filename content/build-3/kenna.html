Deliberation in On Socrates' Personal Deity

My first reading of On Socrates' Personal Deity led me to believe Plutarch was offering a single, correct interpretation of consciousness. After closer examination, I realized Plutarch presents several competing perspectives on how consciousness operates, especially regarding the quality of deliberation. Deliberation refers to the mind’s ability to pause within a given situation and contemplate multiple possible courses of action rather than simply reacting automatically. The essays begin, not with analysis of consciousness directly, but with the rebels gathered in Simmias’ house discussing philosophy as a cover for their gathering. During their debate, Galaxidorus and Theocritus argue over a comical story about Socrates. Their disagreement exposes several different models of consciousness: one in which intellect directs action, another in which logic merely rationalizes non‑conscious processes, and a third combination of the two.

Galaxidorus defends the possibility of authentic deliberation. For him, consciousness is not merely a passive observer but an active agent capable of weighing options. In this view, intellect surveys the causal field and generates multiple possible scenarios. Deliberation is the conscious evaluation of these scenarios, followed by a rational choice among them. Plutarch’s portrayal of Socrates’ daimonion supports this interpretation: Socrates pauses in the middle of the street and chooses to change directions, claiming he made the decision after receiving a godly sign. Galaxidorus insists that such conscious deliberation rests on “reliable experience in some department of common divination” (92). People superstitiously attribute their choices to divine guidance when their decisions actually arise from their own well-trained reasoning. Epaminondas exemplifies this through repeated acts of restraint that strengthen his ability to resist larger temptations. Socrates, Galaxidorus argues, trained his intellect in the same way. His daimonion is not an external force directing him but his own well‑trained mind is capable of interpreting various scenarios. From this perspective, Socrates’ deliberation is genuinely intellectual: he contemplates multiple scenarios stemming from his situation and chooses among them through reason.

Theocritus offers a contrasting view. He argues that reasoning is not directive but interpretive. Consciousness does not generate options; it rationalizes impulses that have already been set in motion by non‑conscious processes. Intellect arrives after the impulse, giving it meaning and narrative coherence. Socrates’ unconscious may have already prompted him to stop walking, and the “divine sign” is simply the story his intellect tells afterward. Socrates behaves like a puppet who mistakes the movement of the strings for his own agency.

Yet this perspective does not entirely oppose Galaxidorus. Simmias, mediating between them, observes that “Socrates’ impulses seem generally to have possessed a consistency and vigor suggesting their origin lay in strong, correct judgement” (92). Socrates’ disciplined refusal of pleasure strengthened his judgment, which in turn shaped his reaction to the impulses he experienced. His deliberation lies not in choosing the impulse but in interpreting it correctly and deciding how to act. Even if he cannot control the initial impulse, he can deliberate about its meaning and choose the best path forward. This reflects a hybrid model of consciousness in which impulse initiates action but intellect guides its final direction.

Epaminondas’ character reinforces this middle position. He believes that through disciplined training of the mind, one can learn to deliberate on impulses by selecting the most rational course of action. Plutarch never explicitly endorses Epaminondas’ view as I initially assumed, but the narrative strongly implies its validity. Epaminondas alone refuses to join the rebellion, honoring his obligation not to harm citizens. His earlier discipline enables him to remain steadfast. Moreover, his intellect allows him to anticipate the consequences of the uprising: he judges that Thebes will be best stabilized by “some persons without part or responsibility for the coup” (112–113). His prediction proves correct when he successfully restores order. In this recent reading I realized through the hybrid model of deliberation, Plutarch implies the existence of conscious freedom. Socrates may not choose the sneeze, but he chooses what the sneeze means. Epaminondas may not choose his initial feeling of obligation, but he chooses how to act on it.

Epaminondas’ actions show that deliberation does not require complete freedom from impulse; rather, it requires the ability to evaluate impulses within a broader field of possible outcomes. Plutarch’s essays ultimately suggest that consciousness is neither wholly rational nor wholly impulsive. Instead, it is a dynamic interplay between the two—a trained capacity to interpret impulses, contemplate alternatives, and choose the most rational action.
