A Phenomenological Experience of Socrates’ Daimonion

In Socrates’ Daimonion, Plutarch presents the daimonion as a phenomenal experience whose significance lies in how Socrates experiences, interprets, and obeys it, not in whether its source is divine, psychological, or the product of his own reasoning. Here, consciousness refers to one’s lived awareness of the world, including the capacity to receive and respond to experiences. Phenomenality, the distinctive quality of what it feels like from an individual’s perspective to undergo an experience, offers a lens for Plutarch’s text. As the speakers’ conversation turns to Socrates’ daimonion and the time when he abruptly turns around because the daimonion “occurred” (91), Plutarch never settles exactly what the daimonion is, exploring instead how it appears to Socrates. Phenomenality clarifies this experience by examining how the speakers’ competing accounts describe a single phenomenal experience despite their disagreement over its source. Simmias’ description of the body and soul establishes that the daimonion cannot be reduced to bodily sensation. Finally, it considers how the daimonion can be experienced inwardly yet as coming from beyond Socrates, demonstrating that consciousness includes the capacity to receive experiences that appear external to the self.

One might object that the speakers are theorizing about causes rather than describing an experience, but each theory rests on a claim about how the sign presents itself. Theocritus calls it a “vision” (91), while Simmias thinks it the “perception of a voice” (104). These are different claims about what the sign is like, with one being seen and the other heard. Galaxidorus, by contrast, treats it as the tipping point of a decision Socrates had already partially rationalized, so that it appears at the end of reasoning rather than interrupting it. This is also a claim about experience, since the sign feels like a culmination rather than an intrusion. The accounts disagree about source and character, but each is organized around the fact that Socrates is stopped, and that stopping is meaningful to him. Their disagreement over the daimonion’s origin therefore leaves the experience itself intact.

Phenomenality also illuminates why the daimonion’s role in Socrates’ life cannot be understood through the body alone. Simmias describes the body as “insensate bones and sinews and moist flesh” that “lie heavy and inert” until the soul conceives an idea, at which point, “the whole mass rises up, taut and tense in every part, and flies into action as though it had wings” (105). The body does not initiate movement itself but is spurred to action through the soul. When Socrates stops and turns, his body performs the physical action, but the significance of that action comes from the awareness that prompted it. His movement cannot explain the experience itself, because it is only the outward result of an awareness that comes first.

Phenomenality likewise makes sense of an experience encountered inwardly yet described as coming from beyond Socrates. The speakers position the daimonion as something he receives that is distinct from his ordinary reasoning. One might object that the daimonion is simply Socrates’ own mind. But this objection concerns where the experience originates, while phenomenality concerns how it appears from the inside. Whether understood as an interruption of deliberation or its culmination, the sign becomes meaningful through Socrates’ reception of it and his response. Plutarch therefore allows an experience to belong wholly to Socrates’ inner life without being experienced as originating in him. Consciousness is not just generating thoughts from within, but also receiving, interpreting, and responding to experiences that appear to come from beyond the self.

The daimonion is phenomenologically meaningful because it contributes to Socrates’ understanding of what it is like to be himself through the way he experiences and responds to it. Although the speakers disagree about what the daimonion actually is, they all attempt to explain the same lived event. Viewed through phenomenality, their disagreement over the source of the experience does not prevent the experience from being analyzed as a phenomenal event. Instead, it draws attention to how consciousness can remain open to experiences whose origins it cannot fully determine. Rather than treating consciousness as a closed interior that produces its own contents, phenomenality reveals consciousness as something that receives, interprets, and responds to what meets it. Phenomenality thus demonstrates that consciousness is not limited to bodily sensation or internally generated thought, but includes what it is like to encounter and respond to experiences that appear to come from beyond the self.
