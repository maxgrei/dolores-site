In my first read of Socrates’ Daimonion, I viewed Socrates as the main character, hedging my entire argument about his experience with his daimonion. I thought the text explored the debate between Socrates’ conscious and unconscious (in terms of the daimonion), and delving into what exactly constitutes free will. However, near the end of my essay, I realized that whether his daimonion is conscious or not, the reality of whether a person can be free could be explained through reconsolidation, which Plutarch opens as a possibility through Socrates and Epaminondas. Reconsolidation is a quality of consciousness describing how one can change their sense of continuity by reflecting on their past, present, and future experiences and incorporating them into their overall vision of self. As such, though an individual may not consciously choose every impulse that directs them, they can still develop a sense of agency by deciding how to understand and incorporate those impulses into their identity. Socrates does not wish for the initial appearance of the daimonion, just as Epaminondas does not pick his poverty. However, both create continuity by consciously reinterpreting circumstances that originally sat outside their control. Their freedom to create their own fate therefore lies not necessarily in controlling what happens to them, but in determining how those experiences assimilate into who they are.

Socrates’ relationship with the daimonion demonstrates reconsolidation most clearly. Caphisias tells a story where Socrates suddenly stops walking, remains silent while “concentrating on his own thoughts,” and then turns around because “the daimonion had occurred” (91). The wording leaves it open ended as to whether his decision is intellectually deliberate or originated from an unconscious or emotional impulse that his conscious mind later interpreted as the daimon. It makes it sound like it is something that happened to him, not something he actually did, like an unconscious thought process. However, rather than resisting or questioning the force that guides him, he comes to embrace it as part of his identity, treating it as something he clearly follows without question. This adoption creates continuity between an experience he did not select and the identity he consciously decides to inhabit. Socrates cannot go back and choose whether the daimonion first appeared, but he can decide what significance that experience will have in his life. What began as something that happened to Socrates becomes something that he accepts as belonging to him and incorporates into the very soul of his being. So, while Socrates may not have consciously caused the daimonion, he still finds a form of limited agency within the process of interpretation.

Epaminondas provides a similar, more concrete example of reconsolidation. His poverty is an inherited circumstance rather than a condition he consciously chooses. He cannot control the circumstances he was born into, just as Socrates cannot control the initial appearance of the daimonion. Beyond that, Epaminondas cannot control the teachings Lysis surrounded him with from childhood. Yet Plutarch describes Epaminondas as taking Lysis’ teachings and using them to consciously reinterpret poverty, saying, “Caphisias and I love these battles, Simmias; and we beg our guest to let us train ourselves on poverty to attain this virtue” (99). Epaminondas transforms poverty and his mindset around it from an external condition into part of the identity he accepts. His poverty builds on Socrates’ daimonion as evidence of reconsolidation, as it is a material and physical trait. Unlike Socrates, where there is uncertainty as to if his conscious mind does have some influence on his daimonion, Epaminondas’ poverty is 100% outside his control. Despite that, he incorporates it into the person he wants to become, creating continuity between his inherited past and his chosen future. He goes as far as to beg Simmias to let him stay in the battle of poverty, framing it as a negative yet something he wishes to keep and will actively train himself in through embracing it into his everyday life. His poverty becomes not simply something that happened to him, but something he rooted in the life he has decided to live.

Reconsolidation effectively complicates the debate of free will simply coming down to the control of our unconscious. Instead, resconsolidation may create a limited, quieter form of freedom by changing the relationship between those causes and the self. Though the unconscious may have initial power, retrospectively endorsing the ideas of our unconscious may be a way to gain that freedom back. Socrates cannot decide whether the daimonion first occurs, but he can consciously accept it as part of himself. Similarly, Epaminondas cannot choose his poverty, but he can consciously transform what poverty means within his identity. In the end, perhaps free will over our actions does not require a complete lack of unconscious influence. Perhaps it lies in the ability to take those desires and reconsolidate them into a new continuity. However, even reconsolidation can be questioned–what if Epaminondas’ acceptance of his poverty or Socrates' endorsement of his daimonion comes from the unconscious itself? In that case, have either of them actually escaped unconscious causation, or merely found a more productive way of living and coping with it?
