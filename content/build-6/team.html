What does it mean to be conscious? Plutarch's dialogue on Socrates' daimonion explores how people can shape their impulses, moving beyond debates over signs and prophecy. Through Epaminondas and Socrates, Plutarch seems to show deliberation and reconsolidation retraining impulses over time, reflexivity taking over when that training fails, phenomenality turning something felt as outside the self into inner experience, and narration taking those changes to create a life of virtue, which together point toward a nonreductive theory of consciousness.

Reconsolidation describes how one can regain a sense of free will by reflecting on past experiences and incorporating them into one's vision of self. Initially, we understood Epaminondas’ repeated refusal of permissible wealth as evidence that conscious restraint could gradually reshape his unconscious desires and make resisting temptation easier over time. However, Epaminondas may have learned to value restraint from past experiences, so we cannot know for sure whether his conscious understanding actually caused him to practice it. Milton’s Paradise Lost complicates this uncertainty, as Eve uses reasoning to justify giving in to temptation. Her example highlights the importance of restraint, since just conscious reasoning may not be enough to resist our desires. Both examples suggest that consciousness does not guarantee control over our desires. We now understand reconsolidation as a process where we recognize how experiences influence us and use that understanding to develop habits that help us resist temptation. Through repeated practice, Epaminondas may gradually develop habits that allow him to resist temptation without having to consciously deliberate each time. Ultimately, reconsolidation suggests that consciousness can influence how our unconscious impulses develop over time, even if it does not fully determine where those impulses come from.

Freedom, therefore, exists on a spectrum and is the product of long-term mental discipline that causes impulses to match deeper second-order desires. Initially, we understood Plutarch’s model of deliberation as one where a well-trained mind rationally evaluates and responds to unconscious impulses. Characters like Epaminondas experience impulses they may not consciously choose and exercise freedom through deliberation before acting. Under this view, conscious freedom occurs through decision-making after the initial impulse and before the resulting action. However, when we compared Plutarch’s work with Milton’s and the principle of reconsolidation, we interpreted that through repeated practice, individuals can actually reshape their unconscious impulses to react with greater restraint before conscious deliberation even occurs.

Consider what happens when Socrates stops in the street; more than deliberation and reconsolidation are at play. Instead, we first read the Crossroads as a moment of reflexivity, awareness of one's own awareness, which is what remains when no sign, habit, or after-the-fact story explains his halt. At first Plutarch's ideal soul seemed only to listen, but we came to see that a soul yielding to a guide has to notice its own impulses shift. Descartes's wax and the rumored sneeze then showed that the senses settle nothing until the mind checks its own state, so one sneeze can push Socrates forward or hold him back. If training shapes most of what we do, reflexivity may take over in the pause where training runs out.

However, this inward check of reflexivity is not one-directional. It is constantly shaped by and projected back onto the world outside the self. Phenomenality reveals that consciousness extends beyond inner thoughts or feelings, developing through interactions with the external world. Socrates’ experience of the daimonion elucidates how something perceived as beyond the self becomes part of one’s inner consciousness through interpretation and response. However, experience does not guarantee understanding. Epaminondas’ discipline illustrates how cultivated character shapes how experiences are felt and interpreted. Scarry expands this relationship by examining how internal experiences take physical form in objects that subsequently reshape the experiences of others. These ideas suggest that consciousness is not strictly internal or self-generated, but emerges as individuals experience and transform their surroundings.

While phenomenality explores how consciousness develops through our interactions with the world, narration considers how these experiences contribute to a larger goal. At a glance, Plutarch seems to suggest that narrative consciousness enables us to choose actions that move us closer to a goal. In Epaminondas' case, this is virtue. However, the daimonion complicates this understanding because its guidance raises questions about how much control individuals have over their lives. By guiding virtuous souls in their decision-making, the daimonion may limit their ability to shape their own life stories. This connects to Plato, whose understanding of virtue also emphasizes the importance of discipline and practice. Both suggest that consciousness can help shape who we become over time, even if repeated practice eventually makes virtuous actions feel more natural.
