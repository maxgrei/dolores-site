Every soul has some part in Intellect. There is no soul without Reason or Intellect, though the part of the soul that combines with the flesh and its passions is changed by pleasure and pain into an irrational thing. This combining, however, is not always of the same kind. Some souls sink entirely into the body, the confusion extends throughout their whole being, and they are at the mercy of emotions throughout their existence. Others merge in the body to some extent, but leave their purest element outside, and this is not dragged down with the rest, but floats on the surface as it were, attached to the head of the sinking man and keeping his soul upright, reining up as much of it as is obedient to its pull and is not under the domination of the passions. The part submerged in the body is what is called ‘soul’; the part that survives destruction is called ‘intellect’ by most people, who believe it to be inside themselves, just as they believe reflections to be inside mirrors. (Plutarch 108-109)

Many see emotions as valuable because of the pleasant memories they have given. But they are also subjective and unreliable, which is why Plutarch believes we may be better off without them. In the story of Socrates' Daimonion, Plutarch encourages people to suppress emotions to cultivate intelligence, using Timcharus' vision to portray emotion as harmful through contrasting imagery and language between destructive emotions and secure intellect.

Timcharus' vision paints emotion as unwanted by associating it with destruction through dangerous imagery and language. Timcharus' vision illustrates that emotion causes the “souls sink entirely into the body” (108). Plutarch is associating emotions with being dragged down with words like “sink”(108), implying a deadly drowning for those who have emotions. While Plutarch certainly does not mean a literal drowning, the underlying point is that emotions will put you on a bad path. Furthermore, “they are at the mercy of emotions throughout their existence” (108) emphasizes emotion's erratic nature. Life controlled by emotion would constantly put one in risky scenarios where they have no control. This portrays emotion as an unpredictable and dangerous tool of destruction that no one would want. Additionally, the vision illustrates that the soul “combines with the flesh and its passions is changed by pleasure and pain into an irrational thing” (108). “Pleasure and pain” (108) are emotions of the body; associating them with a person transforming into an "irrational thing” (108), almost like turning into an unthinking animal, is undesirable. Plutarch clearly views emotions as ruinous, shown through deadly imagery and destructive language. This implies that one should suppress their emotions to avoid such disastrous outcomes. A person who follows Plutarch's thinking would then be unfeeling, but what would replace emotions? While emotions are stomped down for being unstable, what's replacing them is intellect, which is put on a high pedestal for the exact opposite reason.

In contrast to emotions, Timcharus' vision describes intelligence as necessary through imagery and language that convey security. When the vision is describing the souls being pulled down by emotion, it contrasts this with intellect that “is not dragged down with the rest, but floats on the surface as it were” (109), even going as far as to say “attached to the head of the sinking man and keeping his soul upright”(109). Plutarch paints an image of intellect rescuing one's soul from drowning by pulling it up, unlike emotions that would pull it down. Framing the two as opposites emphasizes that intellect is needed compared to emotions. To stay afloat, one would need to get rid of emotions to pursue and obtain intellect. This is further highlighted when the vision asserts that every person has a soul and “There is no soul without Reason or Intellect” (108). By using language that emphasizes necessity, Plutarch insinuates that not only is intellect desired compared to emotions(which are not), but that one is incomplete without it. Another case of language that shows the need for intellect is when the vision explains that while our soul is submerged, “the part that survives destruction is called ‘intellect’ by most people” (109). Intellect is again framed as invincible through language such as “survives destruction” (109). Plutarch stresses how important it is to have intellect, as your soul will not survive without it. The vision through both language and imagery highlights safety and contrasts emotions, establishing intellect as desirable.

From framing emotion as a destructive, uncontrollable force through language and imagery to contrasting imagery and language that emphasizes safety and longevity of intellect, Plutarch shows that unreliable emotion should be abandoned in favor of pursuing greater intellect. But is this what Plutarch believes, or is it only what the vision believes? Throughout the story, there are many different worldviews which offer different philosophical perspectives. It is still possible that Plutarch and the vision's perspectives align. Characters are rewarded for using intellect rather than first judgment or even rejecting desires like money because their intellect determines that they are better off without it. Whether or not the philosophy comes from Plutarch or the vision, one thing remains clear. By emphasizing intellect over emotion, one may navigate the world through objective fact rather than subjective feeling. However, navigating a world without emotions means no joy or desires. Would it be worth making all of the correct decisions if no joy comes from it? Would abandoning emotions make it harder to empathize with others? While pursuing intellect could make one safe, only pursuing it may not be the best way to live. Still, the framework Plutarch provides could help ensure a safe life made with objectively good decisions.
