"Then, my friend, justice also has a way of taking exercise to prevent greed and avarice, not by simply refraining from burgling one's neighbour's house or mugging and robbing people... No; the man who voluntarily and repeatedly distances himself from perfectly just and permissible sources of profit is also the man who is really exercising and habituating himself to keep away from unjust or unlawful gain" (Plutarch, pp. 98–99)

Epaminondas’s speech about exercising justice in one’s daily life outlines two forms of restraint. The first form is stopping oneself from stealing out of fear of facing consequences, what he describes as “refraining from burgling one’s neighbour's house or mugging and robbing people” (98). Epaminondas rejects this form of restraint as a valid way to practice good morals. Instead, he believes that “the man who voluntarily and repeatedly distances himself from perfectly just and permissible sources of profit” (98-99) is practicing a much better form of restraint. Epaminondas’s choice of the word “voluntarily” matters. He is describing someone willing to walk away from a “permissible” profit with no law, no fear, no outside pressure of any kind. Whatever makes him refuse has to come totally from inside his own mind. Epaminondas argues that, in this way, one can train the mind to have more control over impulses and desires, though whether this “training” is a causal process or simply how he explains virtues he already had remains an open question.

In this passage, Epaminondas conveys his view of consciousness through his assumption that repetition of restraint trains the mind, calling it "exercising and habituating himself to keep away from unjust or unlawful gain" (99). He thinks that if you say no to something inconsequential and perfectly legal enough times, such as accepting gifts, one can strengthen the ability to resist more morally corrupt temptations when they arise. Here, Epaminondas is not just making a claim about good morals, but also a larger claim about consciousness. He assumes the mind does not just notice a temptation and choose whether to give in or hold back in the moment. He thinks it is something that can weaken temptations before they show up again. That means that for Epaminondas, consciousness has actual power to reshape the less rational, impulsive parts of someone. This is a big assumption, and Epaminondas never proves this himself. However, Plutarch seems to build toward proving it throughout the dialogue, using Simmias’s account of Socrates’s daimonion and the Timarchus myth as evidence that the inner voice can be a real guiding force rather than superstition.

This assumption also connects to a bigger, unresolved question: does conscious thought actually cause what we do or does the brain and body just do what it was always going to do anyway? Is the experience of “deciding” just something that tags along afterward? Epaminondas’s claim about “unjust or unlawful gain” (99) gives a real example to test this on. If conscious effort can really change a person over time, then his inconsequential refusal to accept gold is building the discipline he later shows when he refuses to kill a citizen without due process. However, if conscious thought does not actually cause anything, then Epaminondas would have ended up disciplined either way. Therefore, calling this “habituating himself” is just crediting good moral character to a training that never actually changed him. The problem is that Plutarch never tells readers which explanation is correct. Those two things happening in order does not prove the first event caused the second.

This same doubt shows up earlier in the reading when Galaxidorus argues that Socrates’s daimonion was never anything supernatural; it was ordinary events like a sneeze or a stray comment that Socrates read too much into. Socrates may have not been guided by an inner voice at all, but simply believed he was in his delusion. Epaminondas’s outlook on his own discipline raises a similar problem. When he claims that “the man who voluntarily and repeatedly distances himself from perfectly just and permissible sources of profit” is “really exercising and habituating himself to keep away from unjust or unlawful gain,” it is a possibility that this really describes a mind reshaped over time through practice. I first read “habituating himself” as proof his mind was reshaped through practice. Now it just as easily reads as a flattering explanation for a virtuous character he already had. If that is the case, then Epaminondas’s whole experience of training the mind is not really evidence of what consciousness is. Rather, it is evidence of how easily people, even philosophers like Epaminondas, mistake a story about their own character for proof of how consciousness works. It’s also possible that Epaminondas was already virtuous and became more so through practice, meaning the story he tells about training himself and the reality of a change in his moral character might both be true simultaneously.
