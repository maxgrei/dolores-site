“The human soul, with all its countless puppet-strings of impulse, is by far the most sensitive machine of all. Handled according to reason, it quickly responds to any inclination and moves in whatever direction the mind conceives. In man, the strings of emotion and impulse terminate in the thinking mind. If this is disturbed, the strings are pulled, and in turn draw and pull upon the man” (Plutarch, 104-105)

A puppet is a motionless, inanimate object, unable to move without the influence of its strings. However, under a puppeteer’s hand it moves, but only within the constraints of the puppeteer’s movements. Plutarch uses this image to argue that emotion holds the same power over intellect that strings hold over a puppet. Human souls are governed by strings of impulse that are irrational, yet not uncontrollable. When those impulses take over, they disrupt our ability to be guided by reason or a higher power. However, the pull between emotion and reason runs both ways, and the mind can act back on emotion just as emotion acts on the mind. This reciprocity makes self-awareness possible, but only with a clear mind. An unclear mind might mistake emotion as rationality, making recognition harder to achieve. According to Plutarch then, self-awareness is one’s ability to recognize when one’s emotions are shaping thought and action.

Later in the text, the voice that speaks to Timarchus describes human beings as flesh, soul, and intellect. Every person possesses reason, but in many people it lies so deeply submerged in the body that understanding becomes irrational. Thus, human behavior is often directed by emotional impulses within the soul, rather than conscious reasoning. The soul, as Plutarch puts it, is “by far the most sensitive machine of all,” making it deeply susceptible to emotion. Once the soul “conceives an idea in the mind and excites its impulses in the direction of that idea, the whole mass rises up, taut and tense in every part, and flies into action as though it had wings” (105). Like a puppet whose strings determine its movements, the body responds to the impulses produced by the soul. However, that relationship can run both ways. If emotion can move the body, the thinking mind can, in turn, move emotion.

That reciprocity is what makes self-awareness possible. A quiet mind not overtaken by emotion is open to guidance coming from a more rational place. Plutarch believes intellect at its purest form is the daimon. The daimon is the part of humans that remains outside of the submerged body and guides the soul. When a person acknowledges that emotion is hijacking rational thought, “Such a soul yields to the guide that slackens or intensifies its impulses, for these are not made violent by emotional resistance, but prove soft and pliant, like reins with some give in them” (104). The shift from puppet strings to reins suggests that reason does not entirely eliminate emotion but can determine how much power we give them. The intellect, or “thinking mind,” is thus able to control emotions by directing and recognizing their influence. However, that recognition is what allows the mind to regain authority over its emotional impulses.

Although it is possible to rein emotion, the problem of false recognition poses another challenge. Plutarch argues that if a person cannot rein in emotion, “the strings are pulled, and in turn draw and pull upon the man,” (105) creating a cycle in which emotion influences the mind, and where a “disturbed” (105) mind allows emotion to have greater control over the body. A disturbed mind loses the ability to rationally evaluate its impulses, so it becomes increasingly likely to mistake emotion for rationality. Therefore, a person may believe they are reasoning clearly while their thoughts are actually being shaped by emotional impulse. Plutarch suggests that self-awareness requires the ability to recognize when emotion is controlling the body, but only a clear mind is capable of such.

Plutarch’s shift from strings to reins outlines his argument on emotion’s power over reason. Emotion moves the mind, but the mind can redirect emotion as well. But if emotion still pulls the strings even when the daimon is guiding the soul, can a clear mind do anything more than redirect an impulse it did not originate? Every action results from a chain of causes, in which emotion pulls on the mind and the mind pulls back on emotion. Recognizing those strings, as Plutarch argues, allows the mind to redirect them, yet does not answer the question of origination. Softening a string’s pull is not the same as stepping outside of the sequence that produced it. Consciousness, then, may be nothing more than the ability to notice the strings controlling us. Whether that noticing can ever originate a new pull, rather than just take part in one already set in motion, remains uncertain.
