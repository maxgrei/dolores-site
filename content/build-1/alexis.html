In Plutarch’s Socrates’ Daimonion, Caphisias recounts how Socrates suddenly stops while walking with his companions and “was silent for some time, concentrating on his own thoughts” (91). Then, as if on a whim, Socrates turns around and changes direction because, as he explains, “the daimonion had occurred” (91). Some follow him; but those that continue on are knocked into the mud by a herd of pigs.

The sequence initially makes the daimonion appear to influence Socrates’ behavior. He is walking, then stops, falls silent, and turns around. If his conscious experience of the daimonion leads him to change direction, consciousness appears to have a role in producing physical action, paralleling Jayne's idea of consciousness.

However, Plutarch’s wording complicates this interpretation. Socrates does not consciously reason his way towards turning around; the event happens “quite suddenly,” and the daimonion “had occurred” (91). The language emphasizes that the experience is something that happens to Socrates, interrupting his existing behavior, not a conscious thought. An underlying unconscious process could have caused both his change in behavior and his conscious experience of the daimonion. What feels like the moment at which a decision occurs may instead be the point at which an already developing process enters consciousness.

This creates a deeper question when considering what counts as Socrates himself. If an unconscious process within Socrates causes him to turn around, it is still a process occurring within him, which begs the question of what we consider free will. Even if his actions aren’t consciously created, they are still driven by unconscious urges unique to him. However, if the self is reduced down to these unconscious processes, then one could pose that we then are servants to our genes and cells – a completely new argument of what counts as human versus mere biological interactions.

Yet, with Socrates, he does not experience the daimonion as part of himself. In a sense, it belongs to Socrates because it arises within his own “consciousness.” In another, Socrates experiences his daimonion as something separate that acts upon him. The passage therefore creates a tension between what belongs to the self and what the self recognizes as belonging to itself. If an unconscious part of Socrates causes his action but he experiences that cause as external, it becomes difficult to distinguish an action caused by “Socrates” from one caused by something acting through him. After all, what is the definition of self but what we ourselves define it as?

This tension is complicated further by Socrates’ acceptance of the daimonion. If his original responsiveness to it is a product of his unconscious, he does not freely choose to follow it. Yet over time, he endorses the daimonion rather than resisting it, accepting the role and actions given to him. In this way, Socrates has found a quieter freedom. While his unconsciousness still steers the ship, he is actively choosing to hold the wheel. Epaminondas provides a parallel example later in the reading. His poverty is something inherited rather than consciously chosen, but he does not remain merely determined by that circumstance. Instead, the text describes how Epaminondas consciously reinterprets poverty as something “easy to bear” and “a dear member of our family” (97). He takes a condition he did not choose and retrospectively accepts it as part of the life he wants to live. In this sense, freedom can emerge through the conscious evaluation and acceptance of those forces. It does not necessarily require creating the initial forces that shape a person.

Ultimately, the passage does not provide a clear answer to whether consciousness causes our actions or merely becomes aware of forces already in motion. Instead, Socrates’ Daimonion suggests that freedom may not require complete control over the forces that shape us. We may not choose every impulse or unconscious process within us, but we can choose how we understand and respond to them. Yet this only moves the problem one step deeper: if even our capacity to accept these forces is itself produced by unconscious processes, then free will may never truly exist. The question is no longer simply whether consciousness causes our actions, but whether a self can ever become sufficiently separate from its own biology to call its actions truly its own.
